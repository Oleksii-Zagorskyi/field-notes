# Surprise hardening—fewer improvisations by whoever acts on your document

**Oleksii Zagorskyi** · October 22, 2026

*This is a pattern for preparing a document that an agent or a person will act on: its shape and the reasons for it. Give it to the agent that prepares your documents with you; the choices it will ask you to make are named where they arise.*

Surprise hardening is the homework—the readings a document gets before it is handed on—that settles on paper the surprises its next reader would otherwise improvise around, as far as reading can reach them. A surprise is whatever that reader meets that the author never registered, or knew and never wrote down, such as a question nobody answered, a step that is missing, or two statements that disagree. A surprise in execution feels like bad luck; often it was homework the author left undone, and that share is the author's to close.

Whoever is handed an underprepared document may stop to ask, or may improvise; either way the result can arrive later, messier or costlier. An agent may also settle a contradiction its own way and move on. The cheapest answer is none at all. Hand the document over and let whoever acts find out, in a trial by fire that turns the one acting into the hero this pattern exists to make unnecessary. Two better answers are older than agents: the author reads it again the next morning, asking whether the work can be done from the page, or a cold reader who knows nothing of the work reads it once. I run the practice below regularly on what my agents carry out (engineering plans, runbooks, operating procedures); on what governs work (policies, company regulations, the instructions my agents work under); on my business decisions; and on what goes out under my name.

## When an agent or a person will act on what you wrote

The practice is built for documents someone else will act on or take as settled—a timetable or an announcement as much as a plan—and building it takes read access to whatever those documents rest on. Your agent—the authoring agent, the one preparing the document with you—usually does the reading; the one acting may be another agent or a person.

The document has to exist in writing, and you need a way to start a reader with none of your context; without it, nobody is left to do the last reading. Worth having, not required: a place to keep the records, and a way to hear back from whoever acts.

## A reader who shares your context reads straight past what the next one will have to guess

Anyone who knows what you meant—you on a second reading, a colleague, an agent that worked on it with you—can fill a gap from memory without seeing it as one, so the document can read complete to everyone except the one acting.

The one acting may never come back with a question; they may spend the run experimenting, improvising and reverse-engineering, and the silence looks like a clear plan. A loose document can produce a loose one beneath it, so in a chain of agents the cost can surface several documents away from its cause.

The pass below—one run of the practice—goes after what those readers miss.

## Therefore—your agent reads it as a peer and as the one who will do the work, hands the last reading to a cold reader, and stops when another pass would only polish

### The homework hunts what the page leaves the next reader to decide, and stops where their own checks begin

The homework puts on the page what the one acting would otherwise decide alone.

1. **Unmarked, a fact, an assumption and a hypothesis look identical on the page.** The reading hunts four kinds of trouble, here at their smallest:

   ```
   contradiction    A < B,  B < C,  A > C       which one is false?
   ambiguity        A is less than B or C        is A < C?
   gap              A = B,  B ≠ C                is A < C?
   unchecked fact   A < B,  while the cited record has A = 5, B = 3
   ```

   Each leaves the one acting to decide what the page owed them: which statement to drop, or their own arithmetic, in which all three hold; which meaning to take; the direction the author never wrote; the page or the record. Your agent checks, read-only, each statement the document rests on; where only running settles it, the document names it as the first thing the one acting checks, with what to do if it is false; otherwise your agent flags it to you—never fills it with a guess.
2. **Every decision the one acting would otherwise make alone, every point where the work meets another system, and every irreversible step in full goes on the page; the detail their own checks will catch stays with them.** Each detail gets one test: would a wrong one build the wrong thing, or would the one acting's own checks catch it in passing? Stop where only the world can answer.
3. **The set of documents handed to a pass decides which surprises are reachable at all.** Before the first reading, your agent asks what each document reads from another's result, and recommends the scope; you choose.
4. **No reading by anyone who shares the author's context counts as a cold one.**
5. **A reviewer that fills every gap it finds hands back a document that reads complete and carries choices made by nobody with the right to decide them.** Your agent closes what the sources answer, as far as you permit; a choice between live options needs your permission, case by case or in advance (below).
6. **A repair is often written under the same partial view that missed the defect.** Each cold reading is a sample, not a sweep: in the planning of one release, which the agents that ran it later audited, second cold readings found blockers mostly in text the first round's repairs had written, and new lesser findings as fast as old ones closed, so the text never settled. What one cold reading misses comes back in the reports. So instead of a second reading, the repairs come to you as a set of changes. Before a finding closes, your agent searches for the old wording, looks for the same defect elsewhere, and traces the repair to every place that carries it out. A repair that reopens a decision or changes the plan—a structural repair—may go, at your request, to a second cold reader of only the parts it touched; whoever acts reads the rest first, with the work at hand, where a wrong repair tends to show up.
7. **A pass with no bound between its readings can become a loop of its own:** each answer launches more work, the work raises more questions, and a pass that finds nothing new can feel like thoroughness. So the bound is on what runs, not on what is said. The decisions a cold reading raises come to you, the document's owner, in one round; what still surfaces is talked through, and no new reader is launched for it. Before another reading, your agent answers a question: would it surface decisions worth making, or only polish? It first states the strongest case for another reading. You decide; as this practice runs, there is never a third cold reading.
8. **A pass that never hears what its document met in use cannot know whether it worked in use.** So each document asks whoever acts for a report.

### The cold reader reads last, after the authoring agent has cleared what it can find

The authoring agent's readings catch the obvious first. Their walk against the sources shows what memory would fill in, and what the sources are silent on is left to the cold reader. For example, a work plan written beside the data model it implements, by the same author, hours apart, read as complete to everyone who knew the model; walked against the model entry by entry, it never mentioned nine of the model's relations.

Your agent calls the cold reader: a fresh instance given the document, the list of documents to consult and a standing brief, the same for every document of its kind, which asks: *could someone with no context carry this out without asking, and would the result be what it says?* It reports blockers, where the one acting cannot go on without guessing or asking, and misreads, where they can go on and may go wrong; a minor omission it leaves out, because the one acting meets it at small cost and its repair would lengthen the document for every later reader. But for a document people read, every question within its purpose that the document leaves unanswered is a finding. For example, the announcement of a new expense policy, written for every employee, goes to one reader given nothing else; any question about the policy that the reader still has after reading the announcement is one it exists to answer, and the list of them is what the announcement still owes. Your agent checks each finding's premise against the documents before adopting it, and records a rejection with its reason, or the next reader raises it again at full cost.

When a cold reader reads is the depth decision: always for a document that changes something irreversibly, leaves your organization, or carries a costly decision; otherwise only when your agent's readings cross a threshold you set in advance, such as two blockers.

### Your agent closes what the documents answer; the decisions come to you

How much your agent may settle without you is a permission you set for each document, and anything expensive to reverse always comes back to you. It may propose every change and edit nothing; close what the documents answer and escalate the rest, each with its recommendation; or also decide what is cheap to reverse, listing each. Start in the middle, move to the last once you seldom decide against what it recommends, and keep the first for anything in your own voice.

### What to do, step by step

Your agent runs the pass in a fixed order; after each reading it sorts the findings and repairs what it may close.

1. It recommends the scope—you choose—and reads the document and every document on its list.
2. It reads as a peer: do the parts agree with each other, and do the steps follow?
3. It reads as the one who will do the work: can each step be done from the page, against every document it rests on, walked in writing?
4. It hands the last reading to a cold reader, where the depth decision calls for one.
5. It repairs what the cold reading found, and shows you every repair as a set of changes.
6. It brings you the decisions in one round.
7. At your request, it calls a second cold reader for only the parts a structural repair touched.
8. It turns findings of one shape into a proposed check.
9. It checks that the document asks whoever acts to report what it did not cover, and, where a person acts, notes who will hear of the surprise.
10. It closes, and records the pass: its scope, the findings and how each closed, what it settled alone, what each reading cost and changed, and the repairs no cold reader saw.

### What comes back from use becomes the next checks

The pass runs without reports from use. They tell you whether the practice works, and turn what the one acting met into lessons for whoever writes the next document about the same work, whether or not that document is ever hardened. Where it can, whoever acts on a hardened document sends back a report of what it did not cover; the one reporting gives each entry one cause, and whoever keeps the practice checks it against the documents of that day, so a miss by the pass can be told apart from a document later work overtook. An agent writes the report as its last step. Where its run leaves an execution trace, whoever checks the causes reads the report against it; a person often writes nothing, so whoever hears of the surprise records it as secondhand. The causes are plain: the answer was knowable by reading on the day of the pass; the document was right that day and other work shifted the ground under it; only running the real system could have shown it; the one acting slipped; someone with the right to decide chose something no record implied.

The reports from many documents are read as one set, later, by someone who acted on none of them—a fresh instance holding only the reports will do. For a miss that recurs, that reader proposes a check for the next pass to start with; for a check whose runs changed nothing the reports show, it proposes retirement. You rule on each change.

### Gotchas

- **A verification that passes whatever happened reads as proof.** Of every check the document relies on, your agent asks whether it could fail at all.
- **A cold reader handed more than it can hold may return a short, confident report that reads as a clean result.** Each reader holds only what its job needs and accounts for every part it was handed; your agent splits a set too large, never shrinks it, and walks the seams between the parts; a part with no line in the report goes to a fresh reader, completing the same cold reading.
- **A cold reader of the same model shares that model's leanings, though not your context.** Where a miss would be costly, a different model, or a person, reads cold.
- **A repair that sits beside what it corrects is paid for again by each later reader.** So a repair replaces what it corrects.
- **A document that names what another has yet to build looks finished and is not.** The first builder may shape those parts differently, as it is free to, and whoever acts on each later document then starts by finding out. Your agent reads the set once for its seams, and each later document opens with a step: read it against what was actually built.

### How you know it is working

The homework is worth it if the downstream reports show fewer and cheaper improvisations than without it. The aim is zero improvisations on anything reading could have answered. What changes in the world after the document was written, or what only running the work turns up, does not count against it.

What to count follows from the causes and the record above: how much was knowable by reading on the day of the pass, and what it cost; how many choices your agent settled alone that you later reversed; what each reading and check cost against what it changed.

My own evidence is the trend in my reports and paired trials, in which the hardened version reached its result sooner or more cleanly—a direction, not a proof. To find out for your own practice, run these; each answers a different question and is blind to something:

- **A paired trial** answers whether the pass is worth it. Freeze a draft; have separate fresh executors carry it out in disposable copies, as drafted, multiple times, and once as hardened, and have someone who does not know which run was which compare what each improvised, what it cost and what came out, against what the pass cost. Only a difference beyond the spread among the drafted runs counts. It is the only test of the pass against no pass. It multiplies the cost of what it samples and cannot reach a document that runs only once.
- **The trend in the reports**, round over round, shows whether the practice is moving—never why a number moved, since every document is different.
- **A labeled test set**—documents with planted defects, at least one for each check, and traps that are not defects—measures each check's recall and its false positives; rerun after every change to the practice, it is the regression test, read against the spread of multiple runs with the practice unchanged. It cannot measure what nobody has named yet.
- **A replay** of past documents through the practice as it stands now measures recall on real misses, and cannot see misses nobody reported.

Which of these to run, and at what scale, depends on your work; they are shapes to adapt.

### When not to build one

A single cold reading may be all a short document needs. A checklist is better for omissions already named—here, it is what recurring findings become. A pre-mortem is better for risks in the world. Fixing it during execution is better when a miss is cheap to repair in stride and the one repairing knows what the author meant—for instance, the author carrying out their own document.

Some documents only look like candidates: an observation, whose gaps are its data; a log, discussed entry by entry; scratch notes; code itself, which has instruments of its own.

Harden a document when someone else will act on it, a wrong turn would cost more than a reading, and the answers they would otherwise improvise already exist somewhere you can read.
